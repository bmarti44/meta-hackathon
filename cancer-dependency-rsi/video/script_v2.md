# It's the Medium: hackathon video script, draft v2

Target: about 2:45 to 3:00. One narrator, subtle music bed, burned-in captions.
Style: Apple keynote restraint and Linear/Stripe polish, with 3Blue1Brown-style step-by-step explanation.

---

## 1. Why this matters (0:00 to 0:25)
VISUAL: Dark screen. A single cancer cell, drawn in light. It splits into a flat sheet, then reforms as a glowing 3D ball.

Most cancer drugs fail before they ever reach patients. One big reason is that we test them on cancer cells that don't behave like real tumours.

So labs started growing something better: tiny 3D copies of a patient's own tumour, called organoids.

## 2. What the studies found (0:25 to 0:50)
VISUAL: Two clean title cards: "Broad Institute, Nature 2026" and "Wellcome Sanger Institute, Nature 2026". A grid of genes switches off one by one; some glow red.

This year, two of the world's leading cancer research centres used organoids to switch off every gene, one at a time, to find the ones a cancer can't live without.

They found hundreds of genes that matter more in organoids than in the old flat cultures. Those genes are now being put forward as new drug targets.

## 3. The catch (0:50 to 1:15)
VISUAL: Split screen. Left: flat cells in a dish, fed a rich red liquid. Right: an organoid, fed a clear liquid. Two labels light up: SHAPE and FOOD.

But organoids change two things at once. They grow in 3D instead of flat. And they eat a different food: a clean, lab-made broth with almost no cholesterol. Flat cells usually get serum, a rich liquid that comes from blood.

So when a gene suddenly matters more in an organoid, what caused it? The 3D shape, which is what we actually care about? Or just the diet?

## 4. The test (1:15 to 1:35)
VISUAL: A third panel slides in: flat cells, fed the clear organoid food. Label: SAME FOOD, NO 3D.

The public data already held a way to tell. Some ordinary flat cells had been grown on the organoid food. Same diet, no 3D shape.

If a gene matters more there too, the diet is the likely reason.

## 5. The headline (1:35 to 2:00)
VISUAL: 17,525 dots fill the screen. 456 light up. Pause. Then they sweep onto a diagonal line as a counter climbs. Hard cut to a huge "85%" on black, music hit.

We ran that check on every gene both studies measured: all 17,525.

456 genes clearly behave differently in organoids. So how many of those also change when you only change the food?

387. That's 85 percent.

## 6. What it means (2:00 to 2:25)
VISUAL: Cholesterol-making genes (SQLE, MVD, IDI1) glow. A cholesterol molecule fades from the dish; the genes brighten.

Most of what looked special about organoids looks like the diet. The clearest example is cholesterol. Starve cells of it, and they have to make their own, so the genes that make it suddenly become essential. Not because the tumour is 3D. Because of its food.

Only one gene leans toward the 3D shape, and even that one isn't solid. A few others, like ITGB1, don't follow the food, and those are the more promising leads.

## 7. How this changes things (2:25 to 2:45)
VISUAL: A drop of cholesterol falls into an organoid dish. The red glow on the genes fades.

A drug aimed at a diet effect won't help a patient. So before anyone spends years chasing one of these targets, there's a simple test: put the cholesterol back, and see if the need goes away. It takes about two weeks.

We also tried hard to prove ourselves wrong. We tested on patients the analysis had never seen, reran it 150 times, and had it reviewed five times. When one early finding didn't hold up, we dropped it.

## 8. The tool, and Muse (2:45 to 3:15)
VISUAL: Screen recording of itsthemedium.com with smooth zooms and a highlighted cursor. Search SQLE. Paste Sanger's headline-18: "5 of your 16 genes look like diet effects." Muse's steps tick in. Ask "Which cholesterol genes follow the food?"

We turned the whole audit into a website anyone can use. Look up any gene and get a plain-English answer. Researchers can paste their own list of hits and see in seconds which ones look like diet effects.

And Muse, Meta's AI model, works like a research assistant. Ask it a question, and it searches all 17,525 genes with its own tools, shows you every step, and links every gene back to the data. Muse also ran the research loop behind the audit itself.

## 9. Close (3:15 to 3:25)
VISUAL: Title card on black. URL. "Built with Muse by Meta."

Organoids are still a big step forward. We just need to check what we're feeding them.
