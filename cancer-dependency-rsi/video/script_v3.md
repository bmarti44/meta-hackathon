# It's the Medium: hackathon video script, v3 (Fable rewrite plus three edits)

Spoken words: 497. About 3:18 at 150 words per minute, plus the one marked pause. Cuts 1 and 2 at the end bring it to about 3:11; all five cuts bring it to about 3:02. One narrator, subtle music bed, burned-in captions.
Style: Apple keynote restraint and Linear/Stripe polish, with 3Blue1Brown-style step-by-step explanation. Nothing cartoony.

Read-aloud notes: short lines are deliberate beats; let them breathe. The one marked pause is the only hard stop in the script.

---

## 1. Why this matters (0:00 to 0:20)
VISUAL: Black. A single cancer cell drawn in thin light. It spreads into a flat sheet on glass. Then it lifts off and gathers into a small, glowing 3D ball.

Most cancer drugs fail before they reach a patient.

Part of the problem is the testing. For decades, cancer cells have been grown flat, in a plastic dish. Real tumours aren't flat.

So labs now grow something closer to the real thing: tiny 3D copies of a patient's tumour, called organoids.

## 2. What the studies found (0:20 to 0:38)
VISUAL: Two title cards: "Broad Institute, Nature 2026" and "Wellcome Sanger Institute, Nature 2026". A grid of dim dots. Dots switch off one at a time; a few flare red.

This year, two leading cancer centres used organoids to switch off every gene, one at a time, and find the ones a cancer can't live without.

Hundreds of genes mattered more in organoids than in flat cells, and those are now being put forward as drug targets.

## 3. The catch (0:38 to 1:08)
VISUAL: Split screen. Left: flat cells in a dish under a rich red liquid. Right: an organoid floating in a clear liquid. Two labels fade up, one at a time: SHAPE. FOOD.

Here's the catch. Organoids change two things at once.

One is the shape. 3D instead of flat.

The other is the food. Cells in a dish live in a liquid called the medium. Flat cells usually get serum, the nutrient-rich part of blood. Organoids get a clean, lab-made medium with almost no cholesterol.

So when a gene matters more in an organoid, which change caused it? The shape, which is the whole point? Or just the diet?

## 4. The test (1:08 to 1:26)
VISUAL: A third panel slides in between the two. Flat cells in the clear liquid. Label: SAME FOOD. NO 3D.

The data to tell them apart already existed. A big public database had flat cells grown on the organoid medium. Same diet, no 3D shape.

If a gene matters more there too, the shape can't explain it. The food can.

## 5. The headline (1:26 to 1:48)
VISUAL: 17,525 dots fill the screen. 456 light up; the rest dim. On the question, the lit dots hold still. Silence. Then they sweep onto a diagonal line as a counter climbs. Hard cut: "85%" alone on black. Music hit.

We ran that check on all 17,525 genes.

456 clearly behave differently in organoids. So how many of those 456 also change when the only thing you change is the food?

If organoids were finding something new, that number should be small.

[PAUSE: two full beats. Screen holds on the 456 dots.]

387. Eighty-five percent.

## 6. What it means (1:48 to 2:16)
VISUAL: Four genes glow: SQLE, MSMO1, MVD, IDI1. A cholesterol molecule fades out of the dish and the four glow brighter. Then three quiet grey dots: SCAP, DDX3X, ITGB1.

Most of what looked special about organoids follows the food, not the shape.

The clearest case is cholesterol. Take it out of the medium and cells have to make their own, so the genes that make it suddenly look essential.

That leaves 69. For 68 of them, the data can't say either way. Just one leans toward the shape, and not strongly.

Sanger headline genes like SCAP, DDX3X, and ITGB1 sit in that unresolved group. Those are the more credible leads.

## 7. How this changes things (2:16 to 2:40)
VISUAL: A single drop of cholesterol falls into the organoid dish. The glow on the four genes fades. Then three plain cards, one per beat: HELD-OUT PATIENTS. 150 RERUNS. 5 INDEPENDENT REVIEWS.

This is a pattern in existing data, not proof. But it points to a cheap test: add cholesterol back to the organoid medium and see if the need goes away. Two weeks. No new screen.

We also tried to break it. Patient data it had never seen. 150 reruns. Five rounds of independent review. One early finding didn't hold up, so we dropped it.

## 8. The tool, and Muse (2:40 to 3:05)
VISUAL: Screen recording of itsthemedium.com, smooth zooms, highlighted cursor. Type SQLE; a plain-English verdict card. Paste a gene list; "5 of your 16 genes look like diet effects." Switch to Muse. Its steps tick in one by one; every gene name is a link.

All of this is at itsthemedium.com. Look up any gene and get a plain-English verdict. Or paste your own hit list and see in seconds which ones look like diet effects.

Muse, Meta's AI model, is the research assistant. Ask it a question and it searches the full table with its own tools, shows every step, and links every gene to the data. And Muse ran the research loop behind this whole audit.

## 9. Close (3:05 to 3:12)
VISUAL: Black. The organoid from the opening, now resting in a clear dish. Title: "It's the Medium." URL. "Built with Muse by Meta."

Organoids are still a big step forward. We just need to check what we're feeding them.

It's the Medium. itsthemedium.com.
